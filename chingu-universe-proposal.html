How We Read The Brief

Three constraints define this project

Everything in our plan answers one of them.

Volume is the real challenge

48 complete stories in six months is not a film problem — it is a systems problem. It is won in pre-production, in a script bank and a repeatable shoot grammar, not on set.

Consistency must survive six months

The same three faces, the same apartment, the same grade, half a year apart. Without a locked bible and continuity discipline, batch 6 will not cut against batch 1.

The turn must be seen, not said

'No Filter Unlocked' is a craft brief, not a caption. It lives in two lighting states, two grades and two editing rhythms — designed once, executed 48 times.

Our answer in one line: Build the universe once — bible, sets, wardrobe, outro templates, beauty shots — then run six identical, low-risk monthly cycles against it.

Our Strategic Answer

One build phase, six repeating cycles, one bank of finished episodes always ahead of air

Phase 01 · The Build

Roughly three weeks, once.

  • — Series bible & 48-episode story map
  • — Casting and chemistry test
  • — Location lock + apartment retainer
  • — Wardrobe & character prop kits
  • — Grade LUTs: Filtered / No Filter
  • — 2D outro templates (2/3/4/5 cast)
  • — Master beauty shot per flavour
Phase 02 · The Monthly Cycle

Identical every month, across four weeks — keeping the crew fast and the cost predictable.

Week 1
Scripts locked & approved — 8 episodes signed off from the story bank
Week 2
Prep & table read — shot lists, wardrobe continuity plan, call sheets
Week 3
3-day batch shoot — ~3 episodes per day, grouped by set-up not story
Week 4
Post & delivery — 8 edits, grade, sound, outro render, subtitles
From month two onward we shoot one batch ahead of transmission. A weather day, a sick actor or a late approval never touches the release calendar — one batch is always in the bank.

Deliverable 5

The Episode Engine

The brief names six friendship situations. Six situations × eight episodes each = 48. That is the whole season.

8
Misunderstanding

Something is read the wrong way and nobody corrects it in time.

8
Hidden Opinion

Everyone agrees out loud. Nobody agrees privately.

8
Small Lie

A harmless lie told to be polite, then defended past all reason.

8
Secret Crush

The one thing the group has all noticed and nobody will name.

8
Awkward Reunion

Someone returns and the old dynamic no longer fits.

8
Group-Chat Problem

A message, a screenshot, a typo, a wrong recipient.

Why this matters commercially
Writers are never staring at a blank page — every brief starts inside a defined bucket. Eight variations of one situation type can be shot in the same batch, on the same set-up, in the same wardrobe. The audience learns the shape of the show, so the 'No Filter' turn lands harder every time, and any episode can be replaced from within its own bucket without disturbing the release order.

Deliverable 5

The No-Filter Turn

The brief asks for a visible transition. We design it once as a two-state system and execute it identically 48 times.

Before · Filtered
CameraLocked-off, symmetrical, wide. Nobody moves the frame.
Lens35mm. Everyone kept in the same shot together.
GradeCooler LUT, lifted blacks, lower saturation.
LightEven, flat, top-heavy. No modelling on faces.
CutLong holds. Average shot length ~4 seconds.
SoundRoom tone only. Clean, slightly sterile.
PerformanceRestrained. Politeness doing all the work.
After · No Filter
CameraHandheld, off-axis, closer. The frame reacts.
Lens50mm and 85mm. Faces isolated, singles only.
GradeWarm LUT, deeper blacks, richer skin.
LightPracticals and neon. Modelled, directional.
CutFast reaction cutting. ~1.5 seconds.
SoundMusic enters. Room comes alive.
PerformanceUnguarded. The truth arrives mid-sentence.

Deliverable 5

Directing Approach

The comedy is in the reaction, not the line. We direct for the face that hears it.

Cast the chemistry, not the résumé

We screen-test in threes, never alone. The audition brief is an improvised version of a real episode — what we're buying is the interruption rhythm between three people.

Shoot the listener

Every set-up is covered for reactions before the speaker. In a two-minute episode the laugh is almost always on the face receiving the line.

Rehearse the turn, improvise the set-up

The no-filter section is run three times: as written, one degree warmer, then unscripted. The third take is usually the one that airs.

Direct in K-drama grammar, deliberately

The push-in, the held pause, the sudden cut to a wide — quoted knowingly, not imitated. The audience enjoys being in on it.

One director across all six batches, with a second-unit AD for pick-ups. Consistency of performance is a directing decision before it is a post decision.

Deliverable 5

Visual Style, Cinematography & Lighting

Polished Korean web drama, adapted for a vertical frame and a scrolling thumb.

Cinematography

  • — Cinema camera, 4K, shot & framed natively for 9:16
  • — Prime lenses: 35 / 50 / 85mm
  • — Shallow depth on singles, deep on group wides
  • — Eye-level for the squad; slight low angle for the sunbae
  • — Product always in the lower third, in focus, never centred over a face

Lighting

  • — Soft key through diffusion, natural window direction
  • — Practicals carry the warmth: neon, pendant lamps, table lights
  • — Filtered state: flat, even, cool top light
  • — No Filter state: modelled, warm, directional, deeper falloff
  • — Two lighting states pre-built per set — the switch is a re-plug, not a re-rig

Grade & Frame

  • — Two locked LUTs applied from Batch 1 to Batch 6
  • — Cinematic but never glossy — grain retained, highlights soft
  • — Safe-zone template for platform UI on every export
  • — Text and payoff cards positioned above the caption band
  • — One colourist across the season, single reference still per set
References: Work Later, Drink Now (tone and drinking-table blocking) · Netflix's "The Swoon" shorts (vertical comedy timing) · Korean web-drama singles coverage · Hong Dae Pocha as the real-world anchor.

Deliverable 5

The 9:16 Frame

Vertical is a composition discipline, not a crop. Every set-up is blocked for a phone in a hand, with platform UI accounted for at shot-list stage.

Framed natively, not cropped

We shoot 9:16 in camera with a 16:9 protect. Cropping a horizontal master loses the faces.

Three people, one vertical frame

Two-shots stack diagonally rather than sitting side by side. Five-hand scenes are covered in singles.

Closer than you think

A 9:16 wide reads as a mid. Our 'wide' is roughly a waist-up, or the group is unreadable at phone size.

Text sized for a thumb

Payoff cards and subtitles are set at a size legible on a 6-inch screen, checked on device before delivery.

Safe zones: top 12% and bottom 20% belong to platform UI — nothing meaningful sits there. Product blocking: faces upper two-thirds, product lower third, in focus, never over a face. Every first cut is QC'd on an actual handset, in the safe-zone template, before it goes to the client.

The brief's hardest requirement. It is answered by documents and discipline, not by talent.

Locked in the build phase and version-controlled. Characters, voice, world rules, what each person would and would not say.

Two graded LUTs plus a reference still for every set-up. The colourist matches to the still, not to memory.

Wardrobe Continuity Plan

Issued before every batch shoot. Per-character colour and silhouette locked; each look logged and photographed.

The apartment is photographed from six fixed marks. Nothing moves between batches without being logged.

A one-page card on every call sheet: the six recurring set-ups, their lens, height and lighting state.

One director, one DoP, one editor, one colourist across all six batches. Continuity is cheapest when it lives in the same heads.

Deliverable 2

Overall Production Approach

Batch production is not a compromise for volume work — it is the only method that protects quality at 48 episodes.

Shoot by set-up, never by episode

Within a batch we group every scene that shares a location, lighting state and wardrobe look — regardless of episode. One lighting rig serves nine scenes across four episodes.

Approve in blocks of eight

Scripts, storyboards and cuts move through approval eight at a time. Fewer approval events, faster turnaround, no single late note stalling the line.

Bank one batch ahead

From Batch 2 onward we are always shooting the month after next. The release calendar is insulated from weather, illness and late feedback.

Keep the same hands on it

One director, one DoP, one editor, one colourist for all six batches — the single largest driver of six-month consistency.

Effect on cost: the same 48 episodes shot episode-by-episode would need roughly 30–35 crew days. Grouped by set-up, they need 18.

Deliverable 2

Casting Strategy

Two male, one female, early twenties, university-appropriate. A hybrid approach: audition for range, filter for reach.

1
Open call
Wide call through university drama groups, agencies and social platforms. Target 120–150 submissions.
2
Self-tape round
One scripted scene, one improvised no-filter beat. Cuts to a shortlist of ~24.
3
Chemistry test
Shortlist run in threes. Testing interruption rhythm and comic timing between people.
4
Screen & product test
Final six on camera in the real grade, holding the product.
5
Client presentation
Three recommended trios with test footage — the client chooses a group, not three individuals.

Why hybrid, not pure creator casting

A creator with a following can carry a launch, but 48 episodes across six months needs actors who can hit a comedic turn on take four at 9pm. We audition for craft first, then weight toward candidates who bring an existing audience.

Contracting the leads

  • — Six-month engagement with dated availability holds for all 18 shoot days
  • — Category exclusivity for alcohol for the series term
  • — Appearance clause: no significant change without approval
  • — Stand-ins contracted per batch as insurance

Reshoot conditions

A reshoot is triggered only by a change that cannot be resolved in post: a rewritten scene after shooting, a product or label change after the batch, or a cast change mid-season. Reshoots are quoted at a half or full day rate and scheduled into the next batch's pick-up window. Technical failure on our side is reshot at our cost.
